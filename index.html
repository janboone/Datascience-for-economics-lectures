Introduction

The goal of these lectures is to build intuition for statistical and machine-learning concepts through simulation. Rather than deriving results analytically, we generate data ourselves, repeat an experiment thousands of times, and read the answer directly from the resulting distribution. This approach — sometimes called statistical hacking — serves two purposes: you always know exactly where your data comes from, and you sharpen your Python skills along the way.

Each lecture starts from a concrete economic or statistical question, introduces the Python tools needed to answer it through simulation, and then explains the code in detail. Interactive marimo apps let you experiment with the key parameters and see the effects immediately.

The lectures build on each other. We begin with foundational ideas in statistics (distributions of estimators, bootstrapping, OLS) and move toward machine-learning techniques such as regularisation, neural networks, and treatment-effect estimation.

Introduction

In your statistics and econometrics courses you have seen analytical results: the sample mean follows an approximately normal distribution by the Central Limit Theorem; the OLS slope has a t-distribution under certain assumptions. These results are elegant but limited. Modern machine-learning models are so complex that no such closed-form distributions exist for their estimated parameters. So how do you assess uncertainty?

The answer is simulation. You write the statistical problem in Python, run it thousands of times, and read the distribution from the resulting histogram. This lecture introduces this way of thinking through three closely linked ideas:

  1. Distributions of estimators — how much does the sample mean or OLS slope vary from sample to sample?
  2. Bootstrapping — how can you test a hypothesis when you have only one sample and no knowledge of the underlying model?
  3. OLS from scratch — how does the optimisation problem behind OLS work, and how do ridge and lasso extend it?

Because you generate all the data yourself, you always know the true answer and can check whether the simulation recovers it. That is what makes this approach so useful for building intuition.

The statistical results in this lecture are probably (hopefully) not new to you. What we want to show to you is the new way (simulation or “hacking”) in which to understand these results. Later on in the lectures there are topics where we do not have analytical results; then simulation is the only option to gain intuition.

If you like this approach to statistics, you can read a free book (Downey 2014) or watch YouTube lectures from Allen Downey and Jake VanderPlas.

Distribution of an estimator

In your statistics classes you have probably done hypothesis testing on a sample mean. Then there was “something” with a normal distribution and “another thing” with \(\sqrt{n}\) where \(n\) was the sample size. Depending on the math content of your course, you have seen these results proved (or not). Here we will explain what these results are and what the intuition is by programming/simulating them. To illustrate, we calculate the mean of \(n\) draws out of a uniform distribution; then we do this, say, 10,000 times. Thus we have 10,000 means and we can show the histogram of these means. What does this histogram look like; put differently, what is the distribution of these means? What is the standard deviation of these 10,000 means?

The clear advantage of an analytic proof is that it shows that the result is (always) true. Simulations cannot prove anything. But simulations can help your intuition and sometimes more than a formal proof. Because we actually simulate the concepts, you can literally look at them. For many students the distribution of a mean is a rather abstract concept. We plot this distribution with simulations making it more concrete. The standard error is then the standard deviation of this distribution. Similarly, the distribution of a slope in an OLS regression may sound vague, but when we simulated the different samples and estimate the slope 10,000 times we can plot these 10,000 slopes.

The app below has two parts. In the first part you choose a sample size \(n\) and a number of simulations \(N\). Each simulation draws a sample of size \(n\) from the uniform distribution on \([0,1]\) and records the sample mean. The histogram of the \(N\) means shows the sampling distribution of the estimator (the mean); the red curve is the theoretical normal distribution from the Central Limit Theorem. When are these two distributions close and when do they seem different?

In the second part you choose a true slope \(\beta_1\), a noise level \(\sigma\), and sample size dimensions; i.e. the sample size \(n\) of the data and the number of repetitions \(N\) of our simulation. The app then runs \(N\) OLS regressions and plots both the histogram of \(N\) estimated slopes and a fan of 50 fitted regression lines. This illustrates that uncertainty about the slope translates directly into uncertainty about where the regression line lies — and that the uncertainty is largest far from the mean of \(x\).

Bootstrap hypothesis test

Above we assume that we know the relevant theoretical (underlying) distributions and draw samples from it. But what if you have a real world dataset and no information on the distribution from which this data is drawn? How can the \(n\) sample with \(N\) repetitions work then? We illustrate this with data from two groups \(A\) and \(B\) and we want to test whether the population means of the two groups are the same. That is, the null hypothesis is: \(\mu_A = \mu_B\).

Suppose we observe data from two groups \(A\) and \(B\) and want to know whether their (population) means are the same. In the app, group \(B\) is generated as \(B = A \times \delta\) where \(\delta \leq 1\), so a smaller \(\delta\) means a larger difference between the means of the \(A\) and \(B\) groups. When \(\delta = 1\) there is no difference; the null hypothesis is true.

The left panel shows the raw samples and their means. The right panel shows the bootstrap null distribution: the distribution of mean differences you would see if both groups were actually drawn from the same population. The observed difference is marked in red; the shaded tails are the fraction that exceed it in magnitude — that fraction is the p-value.

Drag \(\delta\) from \(0.80\) toward \(1.00\) to watch the p-value rise above \(0.05\) as the effect becomes too small to detect with the given sample size.

Simulating sampling distributions

Import libraries and set parameters

We use numpy for numerical computation and matplotlib for plotting. 

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sample_size = 10        # n observations per sample
N_simulations = 10_000   # N repetitions
Generate the data

The key idea: instead of drawing one sample, we draw N_simulations samples at once by creating a 2D array (matrix). Each row is one sample (of \(n\) draws); each column is one observation within that sample. 

samples = np.random.uniform(0, 1, (N_simulations, sample_size))
means = samples.mean(axis=1)

np.random.uniform(low, high, size) draws uniformly distributed random numbers. The argument (N_simulations, sample_size) gives a matrix with N_simulations rows and sample_size columns.

.mean(axis=1) takes the mean across columns (axis 1), so we get one mean per row; one per simulation. Compare this to .mean(axis=0) which would average across rows.

Plot the histogram

Setting density=True normalizes the histogram so that the total area equals 1, which makes it comparable to a probability density function. The alpha parameter controls transparency (0 = invisible, 1 = opaque).

For a sample drawn from Uniform[0,1], the population mean is \(\mu = 0.5\) and the population variance is \(\sigma^2 = 1/12\). The standard error of the sample mean is therefore \(\text{SE} = 1/\sqrt{12n}\). By the Central Limit Theorem, the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately \(\mathcal{N}(0.5,\, \text{SE}^2)\) for large \(n\). 

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.hist(means, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6, color="steelblue", label="Simulated means")
ax.set_xlabel("Sample mean")
ax.set_ylabel("Density")
ax.set_title(f"Sampling distribution of the mean (n={sample_size})")
se = 1.0 / np.sqrt(12 * sample_size)
x = np.linspace(0.2, 0.8, 300)
pdf = (1.0 / (se * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))) * np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - 0.5) / se) ** 2)
ax.plot(x, pdf, "r-", lw=2, label=f"Theoretical N(0.5, {se:.4f}²)")
ax.legend()

We compute the normal PDF manually using the formula \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\). Overlaying it on the histogram checks whether the Normal approximation is good for the chosen sample_size.

Distribution of the OLS slope

To simulate the distribution of an OLS slope, we need to generate \(n\) draws of (x, y) pairs, fit a regression to this sample, record the slope, and repeat this \(N\) times.

Generate data with broadcasting
N_simulations = 1000
n_sample_size = 20
true_slope = 0.5
true_intercept = 1.0
noise = 0.5

x = np.random.normal(0, 1, (N_simulations, n_sample_size))
eps = np.random.normal(0, noise, (N_simulations, n_sample_size))
y = true_intercept + true_slope * x + eps

Here true_slope * x multiplies every element of the matrix x by the scalar true_slope. This is called broadcasting: numpy automatically extends scalar operations to match the shape of the array. In a later lecture we come back to broadcasting in numpy because it is very powerful feature.

Compute OLS slops

The OLS slope formula for a simple regression is \(\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2}\). We can compute this for all simulations at once: 

x_c = x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
y_c = y - y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)

slopes_hat = (x_c * y_c).sum(axis=1) / (x_c ** 2).sum(axis=1)

The argument keepdims=True ensures the mean has shape (N_simulations, 1) instead of (N_simulations,), so that subtracting it from the (N_simulations, sample_size) matrix x broadcasts correctly.

We plot the distribution of estimated slopes (histogram) and the theoretical distribution. If you do not know the theoretical distribution of OLS slopes; do not worry: we use simulations in these lectures, not theoretical results. If you do know the distribution of OLS slopes: why do we plot a normal distribution and not a Student \(t\) distribution? 

# Histogram of estimated slopes
plt.hist(slopes_hat, bins=50, density=True, alpha=0.65, color="coral",
             label="Estimated slopes $\hat \\beta_1$")
plt.axvline(true_slope, color="k", lw=2, linestyle="--",
                label=f"True slope = {true_slope:.1f}")
x_hist_range = np.linspace(slopes_hat.min() - 0.2, slopes_hat.max() + 0.2, 300)
slope_se = noise / np.sqrt(n_sample_size)
normal_slope_pdf = (
    (1.0 / (slope_se * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)))
    * np.exp(-0.5 * ((x_hist_range - true_slope) / slope_se) ** 2)
)

plt.plot(x_hist_range, normal_slope_pdf, "b-", lw=2,
             label=f"Theoretical N({true_slope:.1f}, {slope_se:.3f}²)")

plt.xlabel("Estimated slope $\hat \\beta_1$")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title("Distribution of OLS slope estimator")
plt.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()

Bootstrapping

Generate two groups

We generate the \(A\) sample from a normal distribution. The \(B\) sample is \(\delta \in \langle 0,1]\) from the \(A\) sample. Hence we know that \(\mu_B \leq \mu_A\) (with probability 1.0). Note that we generate these \(A\) and \(B\) samples once. Above we generated these samples \(N\) times; but here we generate the repetitions in a different way. 

sample_size = 50
delta = 0.95         # B = A * delta; delta < 1 means B is lower than A
A = np.random.normal(10, 1, sample_size)
B = A * delta
observed_difference = A.mean() - B.mean()
print(f"The observed difference equals {observed_difference:.2f}")

The observed difference equals 0.51

Combine the samples

Under the null hypothesis (\(H_0\): both groups come from the same distribution); in other words, the group labels are arbitrary. We combine the two samples: 

AB = np.concatenate([A, B])
Run the permutation test

We randomly relabel the combined sample N_boot times and record the difference in means each time. Using np.random.choice we generate a matrix with labels for the permutation. To see what this function does, check out np.random.choice(5,size=(15,10),replace=True). Also try replace=False and see what happens.

For each of the \(N\) repetitions (which are in rows, axis = 0), we calculate the difference in means between the labelled \(A\) and labelled \(B\) samples. 

N_boot = 10000
rng = np.random.default_rng()

perm_indices = np.random.choice(sample_size,size=(N_boot,2*sample_size),replace=True)

boot_diff = (AB[perm_indices[:, :sample_size]].mean(axis=1)
             - AB[perm_indices[:, sample_size:]].mean(axis=1))

AB[perm_indices[:, :sample_size]] uses fancy indexing: for each of the N_boot rows we select sample_size elements of AB according to the permuted indices. In a later lecture we come back to fancy indexing.

Compute the p-value
p_value = np.mean((boot_diff) >= (observed_difference))
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.4f}")

p-value: 0.0063

(boot_diff) >= (observed_difference) produces a boolean array; taking its mean gives the fraction of bootstrap differences that are at least as extreme as the observed one. That fraction is the (one-sided) p-value.

OLS from scratch: the optimization approach

The final topic of this lecture (with statistical concepts you have probably seen before) is OLS. Above we use the analytical expression for the slope of a simple regression (i.e. only one independent variable): \(\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2}\). The same result can be obtained by minimizing the mean squared error loss function directly. This is useful because the loss function can later be extended with penalty terms to give ridge or lasso.

Generate data
from scipy import optimize

sample_size = 500
slope1 = -3.0
slope2 = 2.0
constant = 1.0
noise = 1.5
x1 = np.random.normal(0, 1, sample_size)
x2 = np.random.normal(0, 1, sample_size)
y = constant + slope1 * x1 + slope2 * x2 + noise * np.random.normal(0, 1, sample_size)
Define the OLS loss

The OLS estimator minimizes the mean squared error:

\[ L(w) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(w_0 + w_1 x_{1i} + w_2 x_{2i} - y_i\bigr)^2 \] 

def loss_ols(w):
    predictions = w[0] + w[1] * x1 + w[2] * x2
    return np.mean((predictions - y) ** 2)
Minimize the loss
result = optimize.minimize(loss_ols, x0=[0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
print("Estimated coefficients:", result.x)

Estimated coefficients: [ 1.00394757 -2.96011743 2.05995201]

optimize.minimize starts from the initial guess x0 and finds the values of w that minimize loss_ols. You should recover values close to constant=1, slope1=-3, and slope2=2.

Ridge and lasso penalties

Ridge and lasso add a penalty on the coefficients to the loss function. This discourages the model from assigning large coefficients to variables that have little predictive power; a problem that becomes serious when you have many variables.

Ridge uses a squared penalty: 

lam = 1.0

def loss_ridge(w):
    predictions = w[0] + w[1] * x1 + w[2] * x2
    mse = np.mean((predictions - y) ** 2)
    penalty = lam * np.sum(w[1:] ** 2)   # do not penalise the intercept
    return mse + penalty

Lasso uses an absolute-value penalty: 

def loss_lasso(w):
    predictions = w[0] + w[1] * x1 + w[2] * x2
    mse = np.mean((predictions - y) ** 2)
    penalty = lam * np.sum(np.abs(w[1:]))
    return mse + penalty

The key difference: lasso can shrink coefficients exactly to zero (performing variable selection), while ridge only shrinks them toward zero. Experiment with different values of lam to see the effect. We will come back to ridge and lasso regression in the next lecture.

Summary

In this lecture we introduced statistical simulation as a practical complement to analytical theory. We illustrated the Central Limit Theorem by plotting sampling distributions of the mean, and showed that OLS slope estimates also have a distribution that widens with noise and narrows with sample size.

We then implemented bootstrap hypothesis testing: by repeatedly reshuffling a combined sample under the null hypothesis, we obtained a p-value without any distributional assumption. Finally, we implemented OLS as a numerical optimization problem and extended it to ridge and lasso by adding penalty terms to the loss function.

Key statistical takeaway
When theory gives no closed-form answer -- which is the norm in machine learning -- simulation fills the gap. Generating your own data and running an experiment thousands of times is a reliable way to build intuition about uncertainty, bias, and the effects of model choices.
Key Python takeaway
Vectorised numpy operations replace explicit Python loops and are orders of magnitude faster. The idioms introduced here -- 2D random arrays, axis reductions, broadcasting, and fancy indexing -- reappear throughout the rest of the course.

Introduction

In your statistics and econometrics courses you have learned that OLS measures correlations. A regression coefficient tells you how \(Y\) changes with changing values for \(X\), but it does not say whether there is a causal relationship. In economics, causal questions matter enormously: if you advise the government to adopt policy \(X\) because it will raise \(Y\), you need to be sure that \(X\) actually causes \(Y\), not just that the two are correlated.

Two common but mistaken shortcuts that researchers use when they move from correlations to causal claims are the following.

First, some people select control variables based on statistical significance: add a variable, check whether it is significant, keep it if yes. The xkcd cartoon in Figure 1 illustrates the absurdity of this approach. If you test 20 unrelated variables at the 5% level, you expect one to appear significant purely by chance. The first app in this lecture lets you explore this multiple testing problem directly.

Figure 1: Significance cannot be used for variable selection (in case you need an explanation).

Second, and more subtly, even if you have the right variable in mind as a control, adding it to a regression can either fix a problem or make it dramatically worse; depending on the causal structure. A third variable \(Z\) can play three fundamentally different roles:

  • the fork — \(Z\) causes both \(X\) and \(Y\); failing to control for \(Z\) creates a spurious correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\)
    • a simple example is the correlation between the consumption of ice cream and the number of people drowned on a given day; can you guess what \(Z\) is here?
  • the pipe — \(Z\) mediates the causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\); controlling for \(Z\) blocks the causal path you are trying to measure
    • an economic example: you want to know the effect of education on income; should you control for (un)employment?
  • the collider — both \(X\) and \(Y\) cause \(Z\); controlling for \(Z\) induces a spurious correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) where none existed
    • suppose running \(X\) and cycling \(Y\) skills are uncorrelated in the student population; let \(Z\) denote membership of the university’s elite duathlon team. What is the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) conditional on \(Z\)? Hint: consider a team member with low \(X\).
    • as the collider is the least intuitive of the three, below we analyze two more examples; one with simulated and one with real data.

Each of these three structures is represented by a directed acyclic graph (DAG): a diagram with variable names as nodes and arrows showing the direction of causation. The second app lets you switch between the three DAG types and see how the OLS coefficient of \(X\) changes when you add \(Z\) to the regression.

Multiple testing

Simulate many “null variables”

We generate \(k\) variables that have no effect on \(Y\) and run a multiple regression of \(Y\) on all \(k\) variables. The matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) has shape \((n,\, k+1)\): \(n\) observations in our (simulated) sample, a column of ones for the intercept and one column per variable. 

import numpy as np

n, k = 100, 50    
Y = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
X = np.random.normal(0, 1, (n, k))
Compute t-statistics for the multiple regression

OLS gives \(\hat\beta = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top Y\) and the covariance matrix of \(\hat\beta\) is \(\hat\sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\) where \(\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/(n - k - 1)\) and \(RSS\) denotes Residual Sum of Squares.

For ease of reference we provide the shape of the vector/matrix in a comment on the same line. In a later lecture we come back to matrix operations like multiplication @, transform T, solve and inv. 

Xmat = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X])              # (n, k+1)
beta = np.linalg.solve(Xmat.T @ Xmat, Xmat.T @ Y)    # (k+1,)
resid = Y - Xmat @ beta                              # (n,)
sigma2 = np.sum(resid ** 2) / (n - k - 1)            # df = n - k - 1
cov_beta = sigma2 * np.linalg.inv(Xmat.T @ Xmat)     # (k+1, k+1)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_beta))                      # (k+1,) standard errors
t_stats = beta[1:] / se[1:]                          # (k,); slopes only

The vector t_stats holds one t-statistic per variable, each testing \(H_0: \beta_j = 0\). Under the null, each \(t_j \sim t_{n-k-1}\); for \(n = 100\) and \(k = 50\) the critical value at 5% is approximated by 1.96. 

n_significant = np.sum(np.abs(t_stats) > 1.96)
print(f"{n_significant} out of {k} variables are 'significant' at 5%")
print(f"Expected: {0.05 * k:.1f}")

4 out of 50 variables are 'significant' at 5%
Expected: 2.5

Every significant result here is a false discovery — by construction.

Distribution of false discoveries across repetitions

One run gives one draw from the distribution of false discoveries. To see the full distribution we repeat the simulation \(N = 10{,}000\) times and record the number of significant variables for each draw. 

N = 10_000
counts = np.empty(N, dtype=int)

for i in range(N):
    Yi    = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
    Xi    = np.random.normal(0, 1, (n, k))
    Xmat_i  = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), Xi])
    beta_i  = np.linalg.solve(Xmat_i.T @ Xmat_i, Xmat_i.T @ Yi)
    resid_i = Yi - Xmat_i @ beta_i
    s2_i    = np.sum(resid_i ** 2) / (n - k - 1)
    se_i    = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2_i * np.linalg.inv(Xmat_i.T @ Xmat_i)))[1:]
    counts[i] = int(np.sum(np.abs(beta_i[1:] / se_i) > 1.96))

Simulating fork, pipe, and collider

For all three structures it is convenient to define a helper function that computes OLS coefficients for a simple or multiple regression using numpy linear algebra. As above, the matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) starts with a column of ones (the intercept) and the predictor columns. The estimated coefficients are given by \(\hat\beta = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top Y\). 

def ols(Y, *Xs):
    """Return OLS coefficient vector [intercept, beta_1, ...]."""
    Xmat = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(Y))] + list(Xs))
    return np.linalg.solve(Xmat.T @ Xmat, Xmat.T @ Y)
The fork

\(Z\) causes both \(X\) and \(Y\); there is no direct effect from \(X\) to \(Y\).

As mentioned in the app: if there is an effect between two variables, we assume the effect equals \(\gamma\). Here we set \(\gamma = 1\). 

n = 1000
gamma = 1.0
Z = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
X = gamma * Z + np.random.normal(0, 1, n)    # Z causes X
Y = gamma * Z + np.random.normal(0, 1, n)    # Z causes Y

coef_without_z = ols(Y, X)          # Y ~ X: biased
coef_with_z    = ols(Y, X, Z)       # Y ~ X + Z: correct

print(f"Y ~ X:     coef of X = {coef_without_z[1]:.3f}")
print(f"Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = {coef_with_z[1]:.3f}")

Y ~ X:     coef of X = 0.454
Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = -0.020

The bivariate regression picks up the spurious correlation created by \(Z\). Adding \(Z\) removes it.

The pipe

\(X\) causes \(Z\) which causes \(Y\); the causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) is fully mediated by \(Z\). 

X = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
Z = gamma * X + np.random.normal(0, 1, n)    # X causes Z
Y = gamma * Z + np.random.normal(0, 1, n)    # Z causes Y

coef_without_z = ols(Y, X)     # correct
coef_with_z    = ols(Y, X, Z)  # incorrect

print(f"Y ~ X:     coef of X = {coef_without_z[1]:.3f}  (true total effect ~ {gamma**2:.2f})")
print(f"Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = {coef_with_z[1]:.3f}  (mechanism is blocked)")

Y ~ X:     coef of X = 1.091  (true total effect ~ 1.00)
Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = 0.069  (mechanism is blocked)

An economic example: the effect of schooling on income is partly mediated through employment: schooling helps you to find employment. Controlling for employment makes schooling look less relevant, but the real effect exists; it works through employment.

The collider

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. Both cause \(Z\). Conditioning on \(Z\) induces a spurious negative correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\). 

X = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
Y = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)               
Z = gamma * X + gamma * Y + np.random.normal(0, 1, n) 

coef_without_z = ols(Y, X)     # correct
coef_with_z    = ols(Y, X, Z)  # wrong

print(f"Y ~ X:     coef of X = {coef_without_z[1]:.3f}  (correct, near zero)")
print(f"Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = {coef_with_z[1]:.3f}  (spurious negative!)")

Y ~ X:     coef of X = 0.018  (correct, near zero)
Y ~ X + Z: coef of X = -0.484  (spurious negative!)

The intuition: if we hold \(Z = \gamma X + \gamma Y\) fixed, then a higher \(X\) must be offset by a lower \(Y\) to keep \(Z\) constant. So within any slice of \(Z\), \(X\) and \(Y\) are negatively correlated which is a pure artifact of conditioning.

Finally, we consider the collider in two situations where there is correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\): first with simulated and then with real data.

collider bias with simulated data

Many people have the intuition that adding a control variable to a regression (especially when it turns out to be significant) is a good idea. However, this intuition is wrong if it is applied without thinking. The pipe and collider above are examples where this reasoning is incorrect. Here we consider a collider in a more elaborate example.

We use here the education example by McElreath (2020) to illustrate this point (see Richard McElreath ’s lecture 6). Consider the educational achievements of three generations in one family: the grandparent, the parent and the (grand)child. We are interested in the effect of the grandparent’s education on the educational achievement of the grandchild.

Let’s denote the child’s educational achievement by \(Y\) (the variable we are interested in) and the grandparent’s education \(X\). We want to understand the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\). Clearly there is the following path: more educated grandparent leads to more educated parent (\(Z\)) which, in turn, leads to more educated grandchild. The thing we are interested in is whether there is a direct effect from the grandparent on the child, controlling for the parent’s education.

Figure 2: A collider in a more complicated context.

We know that the parent’s education has a positive effect on the child’s achievement. A more educated parent will tend to stimulate their children more, can help with homework etc. In the data that we generate below, we assume that \(Z = X + ...\) and \(Y = Z + ...\). That is, we assume that the parent’s education feeds one-to-one into the child’s education.

Is it the case that on top of this effect there is a separate grandparent effect? E.g. because the grandparent is babysitting and a more educated grandparent reads Hamlet with the 5 year old grandchild. That is fun and can boost the child’s educational achievement.

Another important component of education is the neighbourhood where the child grows up. We denote this variable \(U\). In the code we assume that the parent and child grow up in the same neighbourhood (have the same \(U\) effect). \(U\) is drawn from a standard normal distribution and the effect on education is given through a multiplication by the factor alpha.

In addition to this, we assume that there is a random component as well which differs between parent and child.

Summary

This lecture introduced two practical pitfalls in variable selection and causal interpretation.

The multiple testing problem shows that significance is not a reliable guide to variable selection: if you test many variables, some will appear significant by chance, with an expected false-discovery rate of exactly significance level \(\alpha\).

The fork, pipe, and collider show that whether to control for a third variable \(Z\) depends entirely on the causal structure. Controlling for a fork variable is correct. Controlling for a pipe mediator or a collider is incorrect; it either blocks a causal path or introduces a spurious correlation that was not there before.

Key statistical takeaway
Significance of a coefficient does not mean the variable belongs in your model. Variable selection should be guided by your causal model (the DAG), not by p-values.
Key Python takeaway
np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y) computes the OLS coefficient vector in one line for any number of predictors. The same matrix operations that worked for simple regression scale directly to multiple regression.

Introduction

In Lectures 1 and 2 we worked with one- and two-dimensional arrays: vectors of observations and matrices with rows as observations and columns as variables. Real-world data often has a more complicated structure than that. An image is a 2D grid of pixel intensities; a dataset of images adds a third dimension for the image index. A panel of incomes across countries, ages and time periods can be viewed as a 3D array. Machine learning frameworks handle this kind of data through tensors; numpy arrays generalized to any number of dimensions.

Three technical tools make tensor computations easy to handle; once you master them. Slicing and indexing let you extract subsets of a tensor in a single expression without loops. Broadcasting lets you combine arrays of different shapes by automatically stretching the smaller array to match the larger one; this replaces many nested loops with a single vectorized expression. Matrix operations are the language of linear algebra: the matrix product @, the transpose .T, and the inverse via numpy.linalg underpin OLS, regularized regression, and covariance matrices.

The second half of the lecture introduces gradient descent, the optimization algorithm inside TensorFlow, PyTorch, and every modern neural network library. We implement it from scratch for a simple linear regression: rather than solving the normal equations analytically, we minimize the mean squared error by iteratively nudging the slope and intercept in the direction that reduces the loss. Broadcasting

Broadcasting lets numpy combine arrays of different shapes by stretching dimensions of size 1 to match the other array. The broadcasting rule: check from the rightmost dimension leftward whether:

  1. Dimensions of equal size: fine.
  2. One dimension equals 1: fine
  3. Neither of the above: error.

A practical example from panel econometrics: individual fixed effects \(I_i\) and time effects \(T_t\), giving predictions \(y_{it} = I_i + T_t\). 

I = np.arange(0, 100, 10)    # 10 individual effects, shape (10,)
T = np.arange(3)              # 3 time effects, shape (3,)

y = I[:, np.newaxis] + T[np.newaxis, :]   # (10,1) + (1,3) → (10,3)
print(y.shape)                # (10, 3)
print(y[9, 1])                # 91 = 90 + 1  ✓

(10, 3)
91

No nested loop over \(i\) and \(t\) — a single expression covers all 30 combinations.

Gradient descent from scratch

The OLS formula \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\) minimizes the MSE analytically. For models with no closed-form solution (like a neural network) we use gradient descent: start somewhere and step downhill.

For simple linear regression \(\hat y = mx + b\) the MSE loss is

\[L(m, b) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - m x_i - b)^2\]

The gradients are

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial m} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_i (m x_i + b - y_i)\,x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_i (m x_i + b - y_i)\]

Update rule with learning rate \(\eta\): \(m \leftarrow m - \eta\,\partial L/\partial m\) and similarly for \(b\). Here we set a seed (42) to make sure that your results will be the same as the ones below. We start our gradient descent at \((m,b) = (0,0)\) and use learning rate \(\eta = 0.3\). Note that we use the derivatives of \(L\) derived above in our updating of \(m\) and \(b\). 

import numpy as np
import tensorflow as tf

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n   = 60
x   = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 2, n))
y   = 0.5 * x + 0.3 + rng.normal(0, 0.2, n)

# OLS:
Xmat  = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
b_hat = np.linalg.solve(Xmat.T @ Xmat, Xmat.T @ y)
print(f"OLS: intercept = {b_hat[0]:.4f}, slope = {b_hat[1]:.4f}")

# Gradient descent starting from (m, b) = (0, 0):
lr, m, b = 0.3, 0.0, 0.0
for step in range(200):
    resid = (m * x + b) - y
    m -= lr * 2 * np.mean(resid * x)
    b -= lr * 2 * np.mean(resid)

print(f"GD:  intercept = {b:.4f}, slope = {m:.4f}")

Summary

This lecture introduced the technical Python toolkit needed for machine learning.

Tensors are numpy arrays with any number of dimensions; .shape reveals their structure, and .reshape reorganizes elements without copying data. Slicing and fancy indexing extract any subset in a single expression. Broadcasting eliminates loops when combining arrays of different shapes.

Matrix algebra underpins OLS and much of statistics: @ for matrix products, .T for transposition, np.linalg.solve and np.linalg.inv for solving systems. A matrix is invertible if and only if it has full rank (det \(\neq\) 0); near-zero determinants signal multicollinearity.

Gradient descent minimizes a loss function iteratively. The learning rate \(\eta\) controls the trade-off between speed and stability: too small means slow convergence; too large means divergence. This is the same algorithm used by TensorFlow and PyTorch to train neural networks.

Key statistical takeaway
OLS is the analytical solution to the same minimization problem that gradient descent solves numerically. Understanding gradient descent makes generalization easier: add (hidden) layers, change the loss function, and you have a neural network trained the same way.
Key Python takeaway
A @ B, A.T, np.linalg.solve, broadcasting with np.newaxis, and boolean fancy indexing are the building blocks of efficient array code. If you find yourself writing a nested loop over array elements, ask first whether broadcasting can replace it.

Introduction

In machine learning and econometrics we face a fundamental tension: a more complex model (more parameters) always fits the training data better, but a model that is too complex will fit the noise in the training sample rather than the underlying signal. That is, the model picks up structure that is specific for the sample and does not generalize to the population.

Adding more explanatory variables to a regression cannot make the training fit worse. The OLS minimization problem,

\[ \min_{\beta} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i(\beta)\right)^2 \]

can only improve (or stay the same) when \(\beta\) is extended. If adding the new variable hurt, we would simply set its coefficient to zero and recover the previous fit.

The problem is that a model optimized on the training sample can pick up idiosyncratic patterns — noise — that do not repeat in new data. We say the model does not generalize. Two failure modes matter:

  • Under-fitting: the model is too simple to capture the true relationship. Both training and test errors are high.
  • Over-fitting: the model is too complex and fits the training noise. Training error is low but test error is high.

The standard diagnostic is a train/test split: hold out a random subset of the data, train the model on the remainder, and evaluate predictions on the held-out test set. The test error is an unbiased estimate of generalization performance. Crucially, the test set should be used once, after we have settled on a final model. Repeatedly comparing models on the test set risks over-fitting to it.

A practical remedy for over-fitting is regularization: add a penalty for large coefficients to the objective function. Two popular penalties are the ridge (\(L_2\)) penalty \(\lambda \sum_j \beta_j^2\) and the lasso (\(L_1\)) penalty \(\lambda \sum_j |\beta_j|\). As \(\lambda\) increases, coefficients shrink toward zero; with a very large \(\lambda\) even complex models under-fit. The penalty strength \(\lambda\) is a hyper-parameter chosen by cross-validation, not by fitting/estimating on the training set.

Ridge regression: shrinking coefficients

Ridge regression adds a squared-coefficient penalty to the OLS objective:

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_\beta \frac{1}{n}\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \]

The first-order condition gives the closed-form solution

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = \left(\frac{X^\top X}{n} + \lambda \Lambda\right)^{-1} \frac{X^\top y}{n} \]

where \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, 1, \ldots, 1)\) so the intercept is not penalized. With \(\lambda = 0\) this coincides with the OLS estimate.

Before fitting, we standardize the polynomial features (not the intercept column) so that \(\lambda\) treats all predictors equally regardless of their natural scale. 

# Standardise columns 1..d using training mean/std
def standardize(Xraw, mean=None, std=None):
    if mean is None:
        mean = Xraw[:, 1:].mean(axis=0)
        std  = Xraw[:, 1:].std(axis=0) + 1e-8
    Xs = Xraw.copy()
    Xs[:, 1:] = (Xs[:, 1:] - mean) / std
    return Xs, mean, std

d = 9

Xte_raw = poly_features(x_test,  d)

Xte, _,    _     = standardize(Xte_raw, fmean, fstd)

def ridge(X, y, lam):
    n, p = X.shape
    Lambda = np.diag([0.0] + [1.0] * (p - 1))
    A = X.T @ X / n + lam * Lambda

Lasso regression: coordinate descent and sparsity

The lasso uses an \(L_1\) penalty:

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_\beta \frac{1}{2n}\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \]

There is no closed-form solution. The single-coordinate minimizer involves the soft-thresholding operator

\[ S(z, \lambda) = \operatorname{sign}(z) \cdot \max(|z| - \lambda,\, 0) \]

which sets the coefficient to zero whenever \(|z| < \lambda\).

Starting from the coordinate-wise objective:

\[\min_{\beta_j} \frac{1}{2n}\|r_j - X_j \beta_j\|^2 + \lambda|\beta_j|\]

with \(r_j = y - \sum_{k \neq j} X_k \beta_k\)

Expanding the quadratic term:

\[\frac{1}{2n}\left(r_j^T r_j - 2\beta_j X_j^T r_j + \beta_j^2 X_j^T X_j\right) + \lambda|\beta_j|\]

Taking the derivative of the smooth part:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_j} = \frac{1}{n}\left(-X_j^T r_j + \beta_j X_j^T X_j\right)\]

The first order condition for the full objective is:

\[\frac{1}{n}\left(-X_j^T r_j + \beta_j X_j^T X_j\right) + \lambda \partial|\beta_j| = 0\]

where the notation \(\partial|\beta_j|\) is short-hand for the following “derivative”:

\[\partial|\beta_j| = \begin{cases} \{1\} & \beta_j > 0 \\ \{-1\} & \beta_j < 0 \\ [-1, 1] & \beta_j = 0 \end{cases}\]

Letting \(z_j = X_j^T r_j / n\) and \(c_j = X_j^T X_j / n\), the three cases resolve to:

\[\hat{\beta}_j = \begin{cases} \frac{z_j - \lambda}{c_j} & z_j > \lambda \\ \frac{z_j + \lambda}{c_j} & z_j < -\lambda \\ 0 & |z_j| \leq \lambda \end{cases}\]

Which is exactly the soft-threshold formula:

\[\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{S(z_j, \lambda)}{c_j}\] 

def soft_threshold(z, threshold):
    return np.sign(z) * np.maximum(np.abs(z) - threshold, 0.0)

def lasso_cd(X, y, lam, max_iter=3000, tol=1e-8):
    n, p = X.shape
    beta = np.zeros(p)
    col_norms = np.sum(X ** 2, axis=0) / n   # yields the c_j
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        beta_old = beta.copy()
        for j in range(p):
            # Partial residual holding all other coefficients fixed
            r_j = y - X @ beta + X[:, j] * beta[j]
            z_j = X[:, j] @ r_j / n
            if j == 0:                        # intercept: no penalty
                beta[j] = z_j / col_norms[j]
            else:
                beta[j] = soft_threshold(z_j, lam) / col_norms[j]
        if np.max(np.abs(beta - beta_old)) < tol:
            break
    return beta

for lam in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    b = lasso_cd(Xtr, y_train, lam)
    n_zeros = np.sum(np.abs(b[1:]) < 1e-6)
    mse_te = np.mean((y_test - Xte @ b) ** 2)
    print(f"lambda={lam:.1f}  zeros={n_zeros}/9  test MSE={mse_te:.2f}")

Lasso sets coefficients exactly to zero (sparse solution) because the \(L_1\) penalty has a kink at zero: any coefficient smaller than \(\lambda\) in magnitude is thresholded all the way to zero. This makes lasso a natural tool for variable selection. Ridge, which uses a smooth \(L_2\) penalty, shrinks coefficients (in absolute value) but does not set them equal to zero.

Tuning \(\lambda\) with cross-validation

The hyper-parameter \(\lambda\) controls the bias-variance trade-off. We choose it by \(k\) fold cross-validation: split the training data into \(k\) equal folds; in each iteration use \(k-1\) folds to fit the model and the remaining fold to evaluate the loss. The average validation loss across folds estimates out-of-sample performance without ever touching the test set.

When you will use \(k\) fold cross validation in your own analyses, you will probably use scikit-learn KFold. Here we program the \(k\) folds ourselves with numpy. Read the code for the function kfold_cv and try to understand how this works (you can ask an LLM for help, if necessary). 

def kfold_cv(X, y, lam_values, k=5, method="ridge"):
    n = len(y)
    fold_size = n // k # integer division
    val_mse = np.zeros(len(lam_values))
    for fold in range(k):
        idx_val   = np.arange(fold * fold_size, (fold + 1) * fold_size)
        idx_train = np.concatenate([np.arange(0, fold * fold_size),
                                    np.arange((fold + 1) * fold_size, n)])
        Xf_tr, yf_tr   = X[idx_train], y[idx_train]
        Xf_val, yf_val = X[idx_val],   y[idx_val]
        for i, lam in enumerate(lam_values):
            if method == "ridge":
                b = ridge(Xf_tr, yf_tr, lam)
            else:
                b = lasso_cd(Xf_tr, yf_tr, lam)
            val_mse[i] += np.mean((yf_val - Xf_val @ b) ** 2)
    return val_mse / k

Bias–variance decomposition

Assume the true relationship is \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon \sim (0, \sigma^2)\) is independent noise. Let \(\hat{y}\) be our estimator. We want to compute the expected squared error at a fixed point \(x\):

\begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\!\left[(y - \hat{y})^2\right] &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) + \varepsilon - \hat{y})^2\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[((f(x) - \hat{y}) + \varepsilon)^2\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})^2\right] + 2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})\,\varepsilon\right] + \mathbb{E}\!\left[\varepsilon^2\right]. \end{align*}

Since \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(\hat{y}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\), the cross term vanishes: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})\,\varepsilon\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[f(x) - \hat{y}\right]\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0, \] and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2\). So: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[(y - \hat{y})^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})^2\right] + \sigma^2. \]

Now we decompose the first term. Let \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[\hat{y}]\). Add and subtract \(\mu\):

\begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})^2\right] &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \mu + \mu - \hat{y})^2\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \mu)^2 + 2(f(x)-\mu)(\mu - \hat{y}) + (\mu - \hat{y})^2\right] \\ &= (f(x) - \mu)^2 + 2(f(x)-\mu)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mu - \hat{y}\right] + \mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{y} - \mu)^2\right]. \end{align*}

Since \(\mathbb{E}[\mu - \hat{y}] = \mu - \mu = 0\), the cross term vanishes, leaving:

\[\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(x) - \hat{y})^2\right] = \underbrace{(f(x) - \mu)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{y}-\mu)^2\right]}_{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{y})}.\]

Combining everything the expected test error decomposes into three terms:

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[(y - \hat{y})^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{y}] - f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{y})}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible noise}} \]

  • Bias: how far the average prediction is from the truth. A too-simple model (underfitting) has high bias.
  • Variance: how much the prediction changes across different training samples. A too-complex model (overfitting) has high variance.
  • Irreducible noise: \(\sigma^2\), the noise in \(y\) itself. No model can remove it.

Regularisation trades bias for variance: as \(\lambda\) increases, bias rises (the model is forced toward zero) but variance falls (the model is less sensitive to the particular training sample). The optimal \(\lambda\) balances the two.

We can verify this decomposition empirically by repeating the experiment many times with different training samples and observing the average squared bias and variance of the predictions.

Why lasso gives sparsity but ridge does not

Both penalties shrink coefficients toward zero, but only lasso can set them exactly to zero. The reason is geometric.

Consider minimising the OLS loss subject to a budget constraint on the coefficients:

  • Ridge constrains \(\|\beta\|_2^2 = \beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \leq c\). The constraint region is a smooth circle (sphere in higher dimensions). The loss contours (ellipses) typically touch the constraint region on its curved surface, away from the axes, so both coefficients are non-zero.
  • Lasso constrains \(\|\beta\|_1 = |\beta_1| + |\beta_2| \leq c\). The constraint region is a diamond with corners on the axes. The loss contours are much more likely to first touch the diamond at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly zero.

We can visualise this in 2D.

Information criteria

An alternative to cross-validation that you may find in the literature is an information criterion: penalize the log-likelihood (or equivalently the in-sample fit) for model complexity directly, without holding out data. Two common choices are:

  • AIC: \(\text{AIC} = n \log(\text{RSS}/n) + 2k\)
  • BIC: \(\text{BIC} = n \log(\text{RSS}/n) + k \log n\)

where \(k\) is the number of parameters and RSS is the residual sum of squares. BIC penalizes model size more heavily (by \(\log n\) instead of 2) and tends to select simpler models.

Information criteria are fast to compute (no refitting needed) but assume the model is correctly specified. Cross-validation makes fewer assumptions and is preferred when data are plentiful. 

def aic(y_train, y_pred_train, k):
    n   = len(y_train)
    rss = np.sum((y_train - y_pred_train) ** 2)
    return n * np.log(rss / n) + 2 * k

def bic(y_train, y_pred_train, k):
    n   = len(y_train)
    rss = np.sum((y_train - y_pred_train) ** 2)
    return n * np.log(rss / n) + k * np.log(n)

plt.axvline(2, color="gray", ls="--", label="True degree")

Both criteria should select a degree close to the true value of 2. BIC will generally prefer an equal or lower degree than AIC because its penalty grows with \(n\).

Summary

  • Over-fitting occurs when a model is so complex that it fits training-sample noise rather than the true data-generating process. Adding polynomial terms always reduces training MSE but eventually increases test MSE.
  • A train/test split reveals over-fitting: if test MSE is much larger than train MSE the model has over-fit. The test set should be used only once to evaluate the final model.
  • Ridge regression adds a squared-coefficient penalty \(\lambda \|\beta\|_2^2\) to the OLS objective. Its closed-form solution \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X/n + \lambda\Lambda)^{-1}X^\top y/n\) shrinks all coefficients toward zero without setting any exactly to zero.
  • Lasso regression adds an absolute-value penalty \(\lambda \|\beta\|_1\) which can be solved by coordinate descent. The soft-thresholding operator sets small coefficients exactly to zero, performing automatic variable selection. A useful rule of thumb: lasso selects at most as many variables as there are observations.
  • The hyper-parameter \(\lambda\) is chosen by \(k\) fold cross-validation, which estimates out-of-sample performance by rotating which fold is held out for validation.

Introduction

In lectures 3 and 4 we saw that OLS, ridge, and lasso all fit a linear model. Increasing the polynomial degree adds flexibility but risks over-fitting; regularization trades off bias against variance to control the over-fitting risk.

Neural networks take this logic further. Instead of manually specifying a feature transformation (\(x, x^2, x^3, \ldots\)), the network learns the transformation from data. Each hidden layer applies a learnable linear map followed by a nonlinear activation function, stacking simple computations to represent surprisingly complex relationships.

For this lecture we move from regression to classification using the Iris dataset: 150 measurements of four flower features (sepal length, sepal width, petal length, petal width) for three species – setosa, versicolor, and virginica. The goal is to predict species from measurements.

A neural network with one or two hidden layers solves this problem well. We vary hyper-parameters like the number of hidden layers, epochs, learning rate, and activation function in the interactive app to see what the effects are on the model’s prediction.

Figure 4: Neural network for the Iris dataset

Figure 4 illustrates the neural network that the app works with. The input layer (left of the figure) has 4 nodes (the four flower features mentioned above) and a bias term (comparable to a constant in an OLS regression) denoted by the \(+1\) grey node at the top. Hidden layer 1 has 5 nodes and a bias node. Each line represents a coefficient. That is, a node in the hidden layer receives input from each of the 4 input features plus the bias. This input is of the form \(w^T x + b\) where \(x\) denotes a vector with the 4 measurements of flower features and \(b\) the bias (constant) term. This input is transformed using an activation function to which we return below. The coefficients \(w\) and \(b\) are referred to as weights. The goal is to estimate the weights of the neural network.

With this vector multiplication, bias and activation function each of the nodes in the hidden layer has a value. Then we do the same again (vector multiplication, bias, activation) as inputs for the second hidden layer. Note that the bias term in the first hidden layer has no line going into it (the constant does not depend on the values from the input layer) but does affect the values of the second hidden layer.

Finally, we do the same from the second layer to the output layer. We use a Softmax function to determine the 3 probabilities that the measurements \(x_1 ... x_4\) belong to a Setosa, Versicolor or Virginica iris.

In the app you can choose between three activation functions which are illustrated in Figure 5. We come back to these functions below. In the app, the Softmax function takes in a vector with three values and turn these into three probabilities: that is, each value is between 0 and 1 and adding the three values gives 1.

The app also allows you to choose two hyper-parameters: the number of epochs and the learning rate. Recall that hyper-parameters are parameters that affect the estimation algorithm but are not estimated themselves; they are “tuned”. The number of epochs refers to the number of times an observation is used in estimation. Training typically requires multiple epochs because a single pass is rarely sufficient for the model to learn complex patterns. Each subsequent epoch allows the model to refine its accuracy and generalize better to unseen data. Tuning the number of epochs is finding the right balance: too few can lead to under-fitting (the model hasn’t learned enough), while too many can cause over-fitting (the model memorizes the training data and fails to generalize).

The learning rate specifies to which extent parameters change given that a change improves the fit (reduces the loss) of the estimation. If the learning rate is too low, it takes a lot of time before the algorithm converges to the parameter values that minimize the loss. If the learning rate is too high, parameters may over-shoot their optimal value and sometimes the algorithm does not converge at all.

Activation functions

Each hidden layer applies a linear transformation followed by a nonlinear activation function \(\sigma(\cdot)\). Without nonlinearity, stacking layers would still produce a linear model. The output layer uses softmax, which converts raw scores into class probabilities.

Hidden-layer activations

Function Formula Range Property
ReLU \(\max(0, z)\) \([0, \infty)\) Fast; sparse; most popular in practice
Sigmoid \(\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) \((0, 1)\) Saturates for large \(z\)
Tanh \(\frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}\) \((-1, 1)\) Zero-centred; also saturates

Output-layer activation: softmax

For a $K$-class problem the softmax converts a vector of \(K\) raw scores \((z_1, \ldots, z_K)\) into probabilities:

\[\hat{y}_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.\]

All \(\hat{y}_k \geq 0\) and they sum to one, so they can be interpreted as class probabilities.

Cross-entropy loss

We minimise the average negative log-probability of the true class:

\[L = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \log \hat{y}_{y_i},\]

where \(\hat{y}_{y_i}\) is the predicted probability for observation \(i\)’s true class \(y_i\). When \(\hat{y}_{y_i} \to 1\) (correct and confident) the loss \(\to 0\); when \(\hat{y}_{y_i} \to 0\) the loss \(\to \infty\).

The key difference between ReLU and sigmoid/tanh is that ReLU does not saturate for large positive values: its gradient is always 1 when \(z > 0\). Sigmoid and tanh both have near-zero slopes when \(|z|\) is large, which causes the vanishing gradient problem; layers receive very small gradient signals and learn slowly or not at all.

Neural network in numpy

We now implement the full neural network in numpy using matrix notation. The network has:

  • Input layer: 4 features (one per iris measurement).
  • \(H\) hidden layers, each with 5 nodes.
  • Output layer: 3 nodes (one per species), softmax activation.

Parameters are stored as a list of \([\mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{b}_k]\) pairs. For layer \(k\) with \(n_{\text{in}}\) inputs and \(n_{\text{out}}\) outputs: weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{in}} \times n_{\text{out}}}\) and bias \(\mathbf{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{out}}}\).

Initialisation. We use He initialization for ReLU (\(\sigma_W = \sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}\)) and Xavier initialization for sigmoid/tanh (\(\sigma_W = \sqrt{1/n_{\text{in}}}\)). Weights that are too large cause exploding activations; too small causes vanishing gradients from the outset.

Forward pass. For each layer \(k\):

\[\mathbf{Z}_k = \mathbf{A}_{k-1} \mathbf{W}_k + \mathbf{b}_k, \qquad \mathbf{A}_k = \sigma(\mathbf{Z}_k),\]

where \(\mathbf{A}_0 = \mathbf{X}\) (the input matrix, rows = observations) and $σ = $ softmax for the last layer.

Backward pass. The combined gradient of the cross-entropy loss and the softmax at the output layer is:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Z}_{\text{last}}} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y}}{n},\]

where \(\mathbf{Y}\) is the one-hot encoded label matrix. For each earlier layer \(k\) we propagate backwards via the chain rule:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}_k} = \mathbf{A}_{k-1}^\top \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Z}_k}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Z}_{k-1}} = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Z}_k} \mathbf{W}_k^\top\right) \odot \sigma'(\mathbf{Z}_{k-1}),\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication. Parameters are updated by gradient descent: \(\mathbf{W}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{W}_k - \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}_k}\) where \(\eta > 0\) is called the learning rate. 


# ── Activation functions ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def activate(Z, fn):
    if fn == "relu":    return np.maximum(0, Z)
    if fn == "sigmoid": return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(Z, -40, 40)))
    return np.tanh(Z)

def activate_grad(Z, A, fn):
    if fn == "relu":    return (Z > 0).astype(float)
    if fn == "sigmoid": return A * (1 - A)
    return 1 - A ** 2

def softmax(Z):
    E = np.exp(Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

# ── Initialisation ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def init_params(layer_sizes, fn, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    params = []
    for i in range(len(layer_sizes) - 1):
        scale = (np.sqrt(2 / layer_sizes[i]) if fn == "relu"
                 else np.sqrt(1 / layer_sizes[i]))
        W = rng.normal(0, scale, (layer_sizes[i], layer_sizes[i + 1]))
        b = np.zeros(layer_sizes[i + 1])
        params.append([W, b])
    return params

# ── Forward pass ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def forward(X, params, fn):
    As, Zs = [X], []
    for k, (W, b) in enumerate(params):
        Z = As[-1] @ W + b
        Zs.append(Z)
        A = activate(Z, fn) if k < len(params) - 1 else softmax(Z)
        As.append(A)
    return As, Zs

# ── Backward pass ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def backward(As, Zs, params, Y_oh, fn):
    n     = len(Y_oh)
    grads = []
    dZ    = (As[-1] - Y_oh) / n       # softmax + cross-entropy gradient
    for i in range(len(params) - 1, -1, -1):
        dW = As[i].T @ dZ
        db = dZ.sum(axis=0)
        grads.insert(0, (dW, db))
        if i > 0:
            dA = dZ @ params[i][0].T
            dZ = dA * activate_grad(Zs[i - 1], As[i], fn)
    return grads

# ── Training loop ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
layer_sizes = [4, 5, 5, 3]            # two hidden layers of 5 nodes each
fn, lr      = "relu", 0.1
Y_oh        = np.eye(3)[y_train]
params      = init_params(layer_sizes, fn)
loss_hist   = []

for epoch in range(300):
    As, Zs = forward(X_train, params, fn)
    loss   = -np.mean(np.sum(Y_oh * np.log(As[-1] + 1e-12), axis=1))
    loss_hist.append(loss)
    grads  = backward(As, Zs, params, Y_oh, fn)
    for j, (dW, db) in enumerate(grads):
        params[j][0] -= lr * dW
        params[j][1] -= lr * db

# ── Evaluate ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def predict(X):
    As, _ = forward(X, params, fn)
    return np.argmax(As[-1], axis=1)

train_acc = np.mean(predict(X_train) == y_train)
test_acc  = np.mean(predict(X_test)  == y_test)
print(f"Final cross-entropy loss: {loss_hist[-1]:.4f}")
print(f"Train accuracy:           {train_acc:.1%}")
print(f"Test  accuracy:           {test_acc:.1%}")

Neural network in TensorFlow with GradientTape

TensorFlow provides automatic differentiation: you specify only the forward pass inside a tf.GradientTape() context, and TensorFlow computes all gradients automatically. This is more convenient than hand-coding backpropagation and scales to any architecture without modification.

The code below replicates the numpy network exactly – same architecture, same He initialisation, same gradient-descent update – but using TensorFlow variables and operations. 

import tensorflow as tf

# ── Define weight variables ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
rng         = np.random.default_rng(42)
layer_sizes = [4, 5, 5, 3]
weights, biases = [], []
for i in range(len(layer_sizes) - 1):
    scale = np.sqrt(2 / layer_sizes[i])   # He initialisation
    W = tf.Variable(rng.normal(0, scale, (layer_sizes[i], layer_sizes[i+1])),
                    dtype=tf.float32)
    b = tf.Variable(tf.zeros(layer_sizes[i + 1]), dtype=tf.float32)
    weights.append(W)
    biases.append(b)

trainable_vars = weights + biases

# ── Forward pass using TensorFlow ops ────────────────────────────────────────
def forward_tf(X):
    A = tf.cast(X, tf.float32)
    for k in range(len(weights)):
        Z = A @ weights[k] + biases[k]
        A = tf.nn.relu(Z) if k < len(weights) - 1 else tf.nn.softmax(Z)
    return A

# ── Training with GradientTape ────────────────────────────────────────────────
optimizer  = tf.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate=0.1)
Y_oh_train = tf.one_hot(y_train, depth=3, dtype=tf.float32)
X_tr_tf    = tf.constant(X_train, dtype=tf.float32)

loss_hist_tf = []
for epoch in range(300):
    with tf.GradientTape() as tape:
        preds = forward_tf(X_tr_tf)
        loss  = -tf.reduce_mean(
            tf.reduce_sum(Y_oh_train * tf.math.log(preds + 1e-12), axis=1)
        )
    grads = tape.gradient(loss, trainable_vars)
    optimizer.apply_gradients(zip(grads, trainable_vars))
    loss_hist_tf.append(float(loss))

# ── Evaluate ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
preds_tr = np.argmax(forward_tf(tf.constant(X_train, dtype=tf.float32)), axis=1)
preds_te = np.argmax(forward_tf(tf.constant(X_test,  dtype=tf.float32)), axis=1)
print(f"Train accuracy: {np.mean(preds_tr == y_train):.1%}")
print(f"Test  accuracy: {np.mean(preds_te == y_test):.1%}")

The tf.GradientTape() context records all computations involving tf.Variable objects. After the forward pass, tape.gradient(loss, trainable_vars) computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every variable in one call – no manual chain-rule derivations needed. This is the mechanism that powers all modern deep-learning frameworks.

Summary

  • A feedforward neural network is a sequence of linear maps and nonlinear activations. The output layer uses softmax to convert scores into class probabilities; hidden layers use activations such as ReLU, sigmoid, or tanh.
  • Forward propagation computes predictions layer by layer: \(\mathbf{Z}_k = \mathbf{A}_{k-1} \mathbf{W}_k + \mathbf{b}_k\), \(\mathbf{A}_k = \sigma(\mathbf{Z}_k)\).
  • Backpropagation applies the chain rule layer by layer (backwards) to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight. For cross-entropy + softmax the output-layer gradient is simply \((\hat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y})/n\).
  • Hyperparameters – depth, learning rate, epochs – control the bias–variance trade-off exactly as in lecture 4: too few layers or epochs underfits; too many overfits.
  • TensorFlow GradientTape automates backpropagation for any forward pass. Keras further abstracts the training loop, allowing the same network to be specified and trained in a handful of lines.

We simulate market data based on the following utility set-up. Consider a product where consumers buy one unit or none at all. Consumer \(i\) buys one unit of quality \(q\) at price \(p\) if

\[U(q, p \mid x_i) = \alpha(x_i)\,q + \phi(x_i)\,q^2 - \beta(x_i)\,p + \delta(x_i)\,q\,p + \varepsilon_i > 0,\]

where \(\varepsilon_i \sim \text{Logistic}(0, 1)\) is idiosyncratic taste noise (McFadden 1974). The parameter \(\alpha\) is the linear term in the utility value of quality. We also allow for a non-linear effect with \(\phi\). If \(\phi < 0\) there is decreasing marginal utility of quality. The marginal value of income is given by \(\beta\). Finally, with \(\delta > 0\) we allow that a consumer derives utility from a high price, especially when the quality of the product is high; a status effect.

The coefficients \(\alpha, \phi, \beta, \delta\) are nonlinear functions of ten consumer characteristics:

Parameter Interpretation Key interactions
\(\alpha\) Quality valuation Elderly \(\times\) health-conscious; outdoor parents
\(\phi\) Curvature in quality Tech-savvy: increasing returns; elderly: diminishing
\(\beta\) Price sensitivity High income \(\times\) tech: low sensitivity
\(\delta\) Quality–price cross Rich loyal: fine with high \(p\) for high \(q\)

These interactions are deliberately non-linear so that a simple linear logistic regression cannot fit the data as well as a neural network.

N_CONSUMERS = 10000

def generate_consumers(n=N_CONSUMERS, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = np.zeros((n, 10))
    X[:, 0] = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)          # age
    X[:, 1] = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)       # gender
    X[:, 2] = rng.beta(2, 5, n)             # income (right-skewed)
    X[:, 3] = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)          # health_consciousness
    X[:, 4] = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)          # tech_savviness
    X[:, 5] = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)       # outdoor_hobby
    X[:, 6] = rng.binomial(1, 0.35, n)      # has_children
    X[:, 7] = rng.beta(3, 2, n)             # education
    X[:, 8] = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)          # brand_loyalty
    X[:, 9] = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)          # time_scarcity
    cols = ['age','gender','income','health','tech','outdoor',
            'children','education','brand_loyalty','time_scarcity']
    return pd.DataFrame(X, columns=cols)

def compute_utility_params(X):
    age   = X[:, 0];  gender = X[:, 1];  income = X[:, 2]
    health= X[:, 3];  tech   = X[:, 4];  outdoor= X[:, 5]
    children=X[:, 6]; educ   = X[:, 7];  loyal  = X[:, 8];  time_s = X[:, 9]

    alpha = (1.5 + 2.0 * health * (age > 0.6)
             + 1.5 * outdoor * children + 0.8 * tech * educ
             + 0.5 * gender * loyal + 1.0 * time_s * income + 0.3 * educ)
    phi   = (0.5 * tech - 0.4 * health * (age > 0.6)
             + 0.3 * outdoor * children - 0.2 * (1 - income))
    beta  = (2.0 - 1.2 * income * tech - 0.8 * income * educ
             + 0.6 * (1-income)*(1-educ) + 0.3 * children*(1-income) - 0.2 * loyal)
    delta = (-0.3 + 0.4 * income * loyal
             - 0.5 * (1-income)*(1-tech) + 0.2 * educ * tech)
    return alpha, phi, beta, delta

def utility(q, p, X):
    if isinstance(X, pd.DataFrame):
        X = X.values
    alpha, phi, beta, delta = compute_utility_params(X)
    return alpha * q + phi * q**2 - beta * p + delta * q * p

def simulate_market_data(consumers, seed=123):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n   = len(consumers)
    X   = consumers.values
    q_offers = rng.uniform(1, 5, n)
    p_offers = rng.uniform(1, 10, n)
    det_u    = utility(q_offers, p_offers, X)
    buy      = ((det_u + rng.logistic(0, 1, n)) > 0).astype(int)
    df = consumers.copy()
    df['quality'] = q_offers
    df['price']   = p_offers
    df['bought']  = buy
    return df

Logistic regression

With a logistic regression model we can write the purchase probability as

\[\Pr(\text{buy} \mid x, q, p) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top [x, q, p] + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\]

where \(w\) denotes the vector of coefficients on the features \(x\), quality \(q\), and price \(p\). The intercept is given by \(b\). Because it is a linear model in the features, it cannot capture the interactions we included in the (true) utility function above.

We read in the data we generated above and select the feature columns we want to include in our \(X\) matrix. We want to predict whether or not an agent bought the product. We split the data into train and test data, 80% and 20% resp. of all the data. To facilitate the algorithm, we scale the \(X\) data by subtracting the mean of each column (variable) and divide by the standard deviation. For this we use the mean and standard deviation of the train data and also apply these to the test data. Below scaler is fit on X_train and also applied to X_test. We denote the scaled version of our variables by X_train_sc, X_test_sc resp. We use sklearn’s LogisticRegression to fit a logistic regression to the train data and calculate our accuracy score on both the train and test data. That is, with logit.predict we predict whether someone bought the product or not (depending on whether probability of buying exceeds 0.5 or not) and then calculate how often this prediction is correct. Usually the accuracy is higher on the train than on the test set. Indeed we choose parameters \(w,b\) to maximize the fit on the train data.

Neural network in TensorFlow/Keras

The following neural network has architecture \(12 \to 64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 1\) (size of input, three hidden and output layers) with ReLU activations in the hidden layers, batch normalization, and dropout for regularization. A sigmoid output gives the purchase probability directly.

The function build_demand_net has three arguments:

  • input_dim=12: the model expects 12 features (\(x,p,q\) in the notation from above)
  • hidden_dims=(64, 32, 16): three hidden layers with 64, 32, and 16 neurons respectively
  • dropout=0.2: a regularization parameter (explained below)

keras.Input declares the entry point of the network: a vector of 12 numbers representing one observation. Think of x below as the data flowing through the network, being transformed at each step.

The for loop builds each hidden layer in sequence. Four operations happen at every layer:

Line What it does Economic analogy
Dense(h, ...) Matrix multiplication: computes Wx + b with h neurons A linear projection into a new space
BatchNormalization() Rescales activations to have mean 0, variance 1 Standardizing variables to make estimation stable
ReLU() Applies the ReLu activation function Introduces the “kinks” needed to fit non-linear demand curves
Dropout(0.2) Randomly switches off 20% of neurons during training A form of regularization, analogous to ridge/lasso; prevents over-fitting

The l2(1e-4) in Dense adds an L2 penalty on the weights to the loss function; exactly the same idea as ridge regression.

The final layer outputs produces a single number between 0 and 1 via the sigmoid function: \[\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\] This is the predicted purchase probability. keras.Model then packages the whole computation graph into one reusable object. As we defined default values for all parameters, we can call the function as build_demand_net().

Experiment with the network by changing values for hyper-parameters like the dropout, kernel_regularizers, hidden_dims and see how it changes the outcome. 

# ── Architecture ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
def build_demand_net(input_dim=12, hidden_dims=(64, 32, 16), dropout=0.2):
    inputs = keras.Input(shape=(input_dim,))
    x = inputs
    for h in hidden_dims:
        x = layers.Dense(h, kernel_regularizer=regularizers.l2(1e-4))(x)
        x = layers.BatchNormalization()(x)
        x = layers.ReLU()(x)
        x = layers.Dropout(dropout)(x)
    outputs = layers.Dense(1, activation='sigmoid')(x)
    return keras.Model(inputs, outputs, name='DemandNet')

model = build_demand_net()

Before training, you must specify three things:

  • loss = 'binary_crossentropy': the objective function being minimized. This is identical to the log-likelihood used in logistic regression:

\[\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\left[y_i \log\hat{p}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)\right]\]

  • optimizer = Adam(...): the algorithm that updates the weights. Adam is a sophisticated gradient descent method that adapts the learning rate for each parameter automatically. Think of it as a smarter version of gradient descent.
  • metrics = ['accuracy']: what to monitor during training (not what is optimized which is given by loss).

Profit maximization

Once we have an estimated demand model \(\hat{P}(\text{buy} \mid q, p, X)\), we can compute the firm’s expected profit at any \((q, p)\):

\[\pi(q, p) = \hat{P}(\text{buy} \mid q, p, X) \cdot (p - c(q))\]

and we assume that the unit cost of producing quality \(q\) equals \(c(q) = 0.5 q^2\).

We find the profit-maximizing \((q^*, p^*)\) by numerical optimization (here using differential evolution, a global method that tends to avoid local maxima). We evaluate the predicted profit maximizing \(p,q\) using the true profit function so we see whether better estimation leads to better decisions.

We make the following comparisons:

  • we calculate the optimal \((q^*,p^*)\) for the true model; if the firm knows the underlying utility functions, this is the profit it would get;
  • we derive the optimal \((q_{lr},p_{lr})\) for a firm maximizing profits using the demand function estimated with the logistic regression; then we calculate what the true profits will be if the firm brings \((q_{lr},p_{lr})\) to the market and
  • we derive the optimal \((q_{nn},p_{nn})\) for a firm maximizing profits using the demand function estimated with the neural network; then we calculate what the true profits will be if the firm brings \((q_{nn},p_{nn})\) to the market.

Try np.full(10,3) to see what np.full(n,q) does. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import differential_evolution

def cost(q):
    return 0.5 * q**2

def profit_given_model(q, p, X, model_type='true'):
    n = len(X)
    X_arr = X.values if hasattr(X, 'values') else X
    if model_type == 'true':
        det_u    = utility(q, p, X_arr)
        prob_buy = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-det_u))
    elif model_type == 'nn':
        X_qp     = np.column_stack([X_arr, np.full(n, q), np.full(n, p)])
        prob_buy = model.predict(scaler.transform(X_qp), verbose=0).squeeze()
    elif model_type == 'logit':
        X_qp     = np.column_stack([X_arr, np.full(n, q), np.full(n, p)])
        prob_buy = logit.predict_proba(scaler.transform(X_qp))[:, 1]
    return float(np.mean(prob_buy * (p - cost(q))))

bounds  = [(1.0, 5.0), (1.0, 10.0)]
results = {}
for m in ['true', 'nn', 'logit']:
    def neg_profit(params, m=m):
        return -profit_given_model(params[0], params[1], consumers, m)
    res = differential_evolution(neg_profit, bounds, seed=42, maxiter=200, tol=1e-6)
    q_opt, p_opt = res.x
    results[m] = {
        'q*': q_opt,
        'p*': p_opt,
        'true_profit': profit_given_model(q_opt, p_opt, consumers, 'true'),
    }

print(f"{'Model':<12} {'q*':>7} {'p*':>7} {'True profit':>13}")

nn_gain_pct = nn_gain / results['logit']['true_profit'] * 100

Summary

  • ReLU basis functions span the space of piecewise-linear functions. Adding more ReLUs reduces approximation error; in a trained neural network the knot positions are learned jointly with the amplitudes.
  • Discrete-choice demand can be estimated as a binary classification problem: a sigmoid output gives a purchase probability, and binary cross-entropy is the natural loss function.
  • Logistic regression fitting a linear relationship misses non-linear interactions between consumer characteristics, quality, and price. A neural network with two or three hidden layers captures these interactions and achieves higher out-of-sample accuracy.
  • Train/validation loss curves reveal over-fitting and under-fitting. Use them to choose depth and training duration.
  • Profit impact: in our example a 3 percentage-point accuracy improvement translates into a 43% profit gain at the optimal price.

Introduction

This lecture covers two complementary applications of neural networks in economics.

The first is empirical: estimating the Phillips curve (the relationship between unemployment and inflation) for European countries. The data are monthly Eurostat observations for many countries over more than two decades. A linear specification imposes a fixed slope, but the true relationship is probably state-dependent: the effect of unemployment on inflation is stronger when the economy is near full employment. A small multi-layer network with tanh activations can pick up this curvature automatically. The key challenge is not the network itself but the data preparation: with monthly observations and lagged features, a naive random split would leak future information into the training set.

The second is theoretical: using a network to solve the Cournot duopoly equilibrium. Here there is no data at all: the network maps cost parameters \((c_1, c_2)\) to quantities \((x_1, x_2)\) and is trained to make both firms’ first-order conditions hold simultaneously. The loss function is derived from the economic model, not from labelled observations. This is a simple example of the broader idea that neural networks can solve equations where a solution is known to exist but deriving this solution is expensive or impossible to compute in closed form.

Why the temporal split matters

With time-series data and lagged features, a random train/validation split would cause data leakage: the training set might contain observation \(t+1\) while the validation set contains \(t\), and the lag features of \(t+1\) include the target value at \(t\). The network would effectively see the future during training.

The correct approach is a strict temporal cutoff: all observations before a fixed date go into training, everything from that date onward goes into validation.

Neural network solution of the Cournot equilibrium

The second application turns the idea of neural networks upside down: instead of fitting a pattern in data, we train a network to satisfy a system of equations derived from economic theory.

The Cournot model

Two firms produce a homogeneous good with inverse demand \(p(x_1, x_2) = 1 - x_1 - x_2\) and quadratic costs \(C_i(x_i) = \tfrac{1}{2} c_i x_i^2\). Firm \(i\) maximizes

\[\pi_i = (1 - x_1 - x_2)\, x_i - \tfrac{1}{2} c_i x_i^2.\]

Taking the derivative with respect to \(x_i\) and setting it to zero gives the first-order condition (FOC):

\[\frac{\partial \pi_i}{\partial x_i} = 1 - (2 + c_i)\,x_i - x_j = 0.\]

The closed-form Nash equilibrium is

\[x_1^* = \frac{1 + c_2}{(2+c_1)(2+c_2)-1}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{1 + c_1}{(2+c_1)(2+c_2)-1}.\]

Equilibrium-solving network

We build a small network that takes cost parameters \((c_1, c_2)\) as input and predicts quantities \((x_1, x_2)\). The loss function is the average squared FOC residual; this equals zero at the Nash equilibrium:

\[\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{(c_1,c_2)}\Bigl[\bigl(1-(2+c_1)x_1-x_2\bigr)^2 + \bigl(1-x_1-(2+c_2)x_2\bigr)^2\Bigr].\] 

def price(x1, x2):
    return 1.0 - x1 - x2

def closed_form_x(c1, c2):
    denom  = (2.0 + c1) * (2.0 + c2) - 1.0
    x1_star = (1.0 + c2) / denom
    x2_star = (1.0 + c1) / denom
    return x1_star, x2_star

net = tf.keras.Sequential([
    tf.keras.layers.Input(shape=(2,)),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(64, activation="tanh"),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(64, activation="tanh"),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(2),    # raw outputs; softplus makes them non-negative
])

opt = tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(1e-3)
The training step with GradientTape

The standard Keras model.fit loop differentiates a loss with respect to network weights. Here we need the same outer gradient, but the loss itself is an economic expression. GradientTape gives us explicit control: we record which computations to differentiate and with respect to what.

The training step uses two nested tapes:

  • The inner tape records how profit depends on outputs \((x_1, x_2)\), so we can compute \(\partial \pi_i / \partial x_i\).
  • The outer tape records how the resulting FOC loss depends on the network weights, so opt.apply_gradients can update them. This we use for the gradient-descent algorithm to minimize the loss.

The loss equals the loss on the FOC’s plus a (small) penalty if price turns negative (this term differs from 0 if \(-p>0\) in the ReLu function). 

def sample_costs(n, c_min=0.0, c_max=3.0):
    c1 = tf.random.uniform((n, 1), c_min, c_max)
    c2 = tf.random.uniform((n, 1), c_min, c_max)
    return tf.concat([c1, c2], axis=1)   # shape (n, 2)

def train_step(cc):
    c1, c2 = cc[:, 0], cc[:, 1]

    with tf.GradientTape() as outer:
        with tf.GradientTape(persistent=True) as inner:
            raw = net(cc, training=True)
            x1  = tf.nn.softplus(raw[:, 0])   # softplus ensures xi >= 0
            x2  = tf.nn.softplus(raw[:, 1])

            inner.watch([x1, x2])             # track x1, x2 for profit derivatives
            p   = price(x1, x2)
            pi1 = p * x1 - 0.5 * c1 * tf.square(x1)
            pi2 = p * x2 - 0.5 * c2 * tf.square(x2)

        r1 = inner.gradient(pi1, x1)   # ∂π1/∂x1 (should be 0 at equilibrium)
        r2 = inner.gradient(pi2, x2)   # ∂π2/∂x2
        del inner

        # loss function has two components
        loss  = tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(r1)) + tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(r2))
        loss += 1e-2 * tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(tf.nn.relu(-p)))

    grads = outer.gradient(loss, net.trainable_variables)
    opt.apply_gradients(zip(grads, net.trainable_variables))
    return loss

The key line is inner.watch([x1, x2]). By default GradientTape only tracks tf.Variable objects. Because x1 and x2 are computed tensors (not variables), we must explicitly tell the inner tape to watch them; otherwise inner.gradient(pi1, x1) returns None.

The inner tape is created with persistent = True. By default a GradientTape frees all its recorded computation as soon as .gradient() is called once; it assumes you only need one gradient per tape. persistent = True disables that automatic release so the tape can be queried multiple times. Here we need it twice:

  • r1 = inner.gradient(pi1, x1) and
  • r2 = inner.gradient(pi2, x2)

Without persistent = True the second call would return None because the tape’s resources would already be gone after the first.

The cost is that a persistent tape holds the entire recorded computation graph in memory indefinitely. Inside a loop of 1500 iterations that accumulates fast. del inner forces the release immediately after the two gradient calls, before the outer tape computes its own gradient.

Training loop

We train over 1500 iterations and every 300 iterations print the results on the test data. 

for it in range(1, 1501):
    cc = sample_costs(512)
    loss = train_step(cc)

    if it % 300 == 0:
        cc_test         = sample_costs(256)
        raw_test        = net(cc_test, training=False)
        x1_test         = tf.nn.softplus(raw_test[:, 0])
        x2_test         = tf.nn.softplus(raw_test[:, 1])
        x1_star, x2_star = closed_form_x(cc_test[:, 0], cc_test[:, 1])
        mae = (tf.reduce_mean(tf.abs(x1_test - x1_star)) +
               tf.reduce_mean(tf.abs(x2_test - x2_star)))
        print(f"iter {it:4d} | loss {loss.numpy():.3e} | test MAE {mae.numpy():.3e}")

Summary

  • Time-series panel data requires a strict temporal train/validation split. Using a random split with lagged features leaks the future into training and gives over-optimistic validation scores.
  • tanh activations are a natural choice for regression targets that can be positive or negative and for relationships expected to be smooth and symmetric.
  • Standardization must always be done with training statistics; applying the same (training) transformation to the validation set is correct and does not constitute leakage.
  • A neural network can solve for economic equilibria without any labelled data by minimizing the squared first-order conditions. The loss function encodes economic theory rather than prediction error.
  • TensorFlow’s GradientTape gives fine-grained control over differentiation and is essential when the loss depends on derivatives of the model’s outputs with respect to its inputs.

Course takeaway: simulation as a bridge to machine learning

These lectures show that the gap between classical econometrics and modern machine learning is smaller than it appears. OLS, the workhorse of econometrics, is just a specific loss function minimised by a simple algorithm. Ridge and lasso are OLS with an extra line of code. Neural networks are deeper versions of the same idea.

By building everything from scratch using simulation and optimisation, you develop a type of understanding that no amount of reading about formulas can give. You see directly what the parameters do, how the estimates vary, and what happens when assumptions break down.
